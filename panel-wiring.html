Panel Wiring

Every connection, the order to make them, and the two things that were missing.

⛔ Two things to fix before any wire is landed

1. There was no grounding in this design. Two steel boxes with 120V inside, on a wooden deck rail, with no path to earth. A hot conductor chafing an enclosure wall would make the whole box live at 120V with nothing to trip it. Add the ground bar below before AC comes near these boxes.

2. Do not use a Cat5 cable for the Tycon RS485. The Tycon's RJ45 carries +7V on pin 7 and +5V on pin 8. The TPDIN's port is RJ12, 6 pin, and those land on its RS232 receive and transmit pins. A straight cable feeds power into a serial input and kills it. Buy the Tycon TPDIN-CABLE-485 (not the -232, that is for the older 20N/40N).

The grounding plan (new)

One rod, star topology, one DC bond

Everything metal bonds back to a single ground bar in Box B, and that bar runs to one ground rod. No loops, no second electrode, no daisy chains.

  1. Ground bar in Box B. A small lay-in bus. Bond to it: the box body, the metal back panel, the door (flexible braided jumper if there is no factory strap), the DIN rail, the Tycon GND terminal, and the Iota chassis. Scrape paint or use paint-piercing star washers at every point, these boxes are painted and paint is an insulator.
  2. 10 AWG green up to Box A, bonding its body, panel, door, and the inverter chassis lug.
  3. 6 AWG from the ground bar to one ground rod near the stack.
  4. The DC system bond: exactly ONE. A 6 AWG jumper from the deck box negative bus to the ground. Nowhere else. Not at the array, not at the Tycon, not at the inverter. Label that jumper so nobody ever adds a second one.
  5. The array rail and the DIHOOL enclosure bond to the same rod. One electrode system, not two.

Three grounding gotchas

Bond to the ROD, not to the trailer. Your house is on wheels. Keep the ground path independent of it, and put "disconnect the array down-conductor and the EGC" on a move checklist.

Use green insulated 6 AWG for the array down-run, not bare copper. Bare copper against aluminum rail or a metal roof is a galvanic corrosion problem. Use an AL9CU dual-rated lug with antioxidant at the rail.

The Tycon has no ground fault detection. Its manual says so outright. An array-to-ground fault will not be caught by anything in this system, which is exactly why the bonding has to be right.

Two AC blockers in Box A

The output had no overcurrent protection. Terminal block straight to an outlet leaves the branch conductor unprotected. Add a 20A single-pole breaker (or inline AC fuse) at the inverter output and run 12 AWG.

Measure the neutral bonding before you wire the GFCI. With the inverter running and nothing plugged in, read L to G and N to G.
120V and 0V = internally bonded, change nothing, the GFCI will work.
About 60V on both = floating, and a GFCI will not protect anyone until you create one neutral-to-ground bond at the inverter's terminal block. One place only, never also at the receptacle.

Box B corrections

PV wire goes to 8 AWG on the combined run from the Y-branch through the DIHOOL to the Tycon. Three strings combined is too much for 10 AWG. Per-string 10 AWG is still fine.

Check your panel labels for "max series fuse rating." With three parallel strings, a fault in one can be backfed by the other two at about 22A. If the label says 15A (likely), you need an inline fuse in each string positive before the Y-branch.

Add a manual AC disconnect upstream of the contactor, or feed Box B cord-and-plug, so you can kill grid power locally instead of walking to the house panel.

Use a normally-open TPDIN channel for the coil, so losing the TPDIN means no grid charging rather than charging forever.

Segregate the low voltage. Keep the Cat5 and the coil pair on the opposite side of the panel from the 120V, no crossings.

Do not install the free dual-voltage plug and the IQ-LIFEPO together. IQ module only, set the plug aside.

The 70A fuse on the Tycon lead is confirmed correct: the datasheet calls for 63A to 100A. Ignore any note saying 100A minimum.

Commissioning, in this order

Battery first, PV last. Always.

The Tycon is 12/24V auto-detect and reads battery voltage at power-up to choose its system voltage. Its manual: first connect the battery, then the load, and finally the solar panel.

Disconnect is the exact reverse: PV first, battery last. Never break the battery connection while PV is live.

  1. Grounding first. Ground bar, all bonds, rod, and the single DC negative bond. Before anything is energized.
  2. Everything open: DIHOOL open, 70A fuse out of its holder, 30A out, inverter breaker off, grid disconnect off.
  3. Land the battery leads at the Tycon (negative then positive), torque to 1.7 N·m.
  4. Insert the 70A fuse. The controller boots off the battery. Set the LiFePO4 24V preset, confirm 28.8V on screen, disable temperature compensation. Do not leave it on auto.
  5. Verify polarity at the inverter (red probe on +, black on −, must read positive ~26V), pre-charge the caps with a 10 to 50 ohm resistor until within 0.5V of bank voltage, then close the 80A breaker.
  6. Insert the 30A fuse for the Iota. Then energize grid AC and watch one full automatic charge cycle before walking away.
  7. PV LAST. Land the array, then close the DIHOOL. Confirm the controller shows charging.
  8. First high-load run: IR-sweep every lug, both boxes, and the bus. Re-torque afterward. Confirm Box B air stays well under its limit.

Corrections to earlier guidance

"Set the Tycon to Lithium BEFORE connecting the batteries" was impossible as written. It needs battery power to configure. The correct rule is: battery connected, PV disconnected, then configure, then land PV.

The 70A fuse is right (63 to 100A band per the datasheet).

2S3P is confirmed at Tycon's stated maximum series count for a 100V unit. The array is 1500W against a 1560W limit, so it cannot grow on this controller.

Do not open the inverter to tap the rocker for remote control. Check for a factory remote port first; if there is none, the right answer at this scale is a DC contactor in the battery feed, not a tap inside the case.

Where to get a second opinion

The DC side is well within competent DIY territory. The AC side is where a licensed electrician is worth the call: the neutral bonding decision, the branch breaker, the GFCI, and the grounding electrode. It is one visit and it is the part where being wrong hurts someone.
