Gland Plan

Where each cable enters, and what size gland it needs.

Glands for the DC, conduit for the AC

Use cable glands for everything on the DC side. Your 2/0, 6 ga, and PV wire all have jackets already rated for exposed outdoor use, the runs are short and up off the ground, and a gland gives you both the IP65 seal and strain relief in one part. 2/0 in conduit would need 1¼ to 1½″ for a pair, which is stiff, expensive, and as you found, will not fit.

Use conduit for the AC run to your office. That one is a branch circuit crossing a steel penetration into a living space, so it wants real mechanical protection and it is the code-appropriate choice. Small wire pulls easily, so conduit costs you nothing there.

Conduit hubs are for pulling loose conductors (individual THWN). You are running jacketed cable, so glands are the natural match. Mixing the two on one box is completely normal.

The quad glands are the win here

Those 4-port Mikrotik glands let you put RS485, the TPDIN relay pair, and two spares through ONE hole instead of four. That is three fewer penetrations to drill in 3/8″ steel, and three fewer things that can leak. Use one in Box B for all the small stuff and keep the other two for the irrigation control box later.

The one thing to buy: two 1″ NPT glands

2/0 at 0.60 to 0.65″ is above what a 3/4″ NPT gland typically clamps (most stop around 0.55″). You need 1″ NPT, which clamps roughly 0.51 to 0.75″.

Test before you order: you have a spare 3/4″. Push actual 2/0 through it, tighten the nut hard, and pull. If the rubber genuinely bites and the cable will not budge, you only need to buy one. If the nut bottoms out before gripping, buy two. Do not force it, a gland that does not compress is just a hole.

Two cautions on reusing these

Your 1/2″ stock is exactly consumed. Two glands for the two 6 ga Tycon leads, no spare. If you strip one, you are stopped, so pick up a couple extra 1/2″ while you are buying the 1″ ones.

Confirm every gland still has its locknut and gasket. NPT glands are made to thread into a tapped port; in a plain drilled panel hole the seal comes from the locknut plus the washer or O-ring under the head. A gland with a missing locknut is not watertight, and pulled-from-a-router parts often lose them.

Rules that keep these watertight

  1. One cable per gland. Two cables squeezed into one gland will not seal, and it voids the IP rating. Blank any hole you are not using.
  2. Match the gland to the cable OD, not the wire gauge. A gland only seals inside its clamping range. Too big and it never grips, too small and you cannot close it. Measure the actual jacket with your calipers before ordering.
  3. Drip loop below every gland. The cable should hang below the entry before rising. Water follows cable, and a drip loop gives it somewhere to fall off.
  4. Deburr and paint every hole. These are painted steel boxes. A bare drilled edge is where rust starts, and this is a wet, sun-exposed location.
  5. Tighten the compression nut on the cable, not just the body. An untightened gland is just a hole with a fitting in it. You should not be able to pull the cable through by hand.

On running the 2/0 between the boxes

It would work mechanically, but that gap is where the lower box's top-face fan discharges. It is already restricted by how close the boxes ended up, and packing 2/0 in there makes it worse. The fan is what is buying you the reduced clearances inside.

Route the 2/0 up the outside right instead, into the two 1″ glands low on Box A. If you do end up using the gap, keep it to one cable and shove it to one side so the fan still has a path.

Two positioning notes

Box A, the 2/0 goes LOW and the AC goes HIGH. That is set by the inverter: DC studs at the bottom, AC receptacles at the top. It keeps interior cable short, which matters in a box where the inverter leaves under an inch of side clearance.

Box B, PV enters high (it comes down from the disconnect above) while the deck-box runs enter mid and low (they go down). Grid AC and data sit lowest, away from everything else.
