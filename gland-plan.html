Drilling these is the easy part

The box walls are thin sheet steel, not the 3/8" plate on your wheel well. A step bit walks right through them. Only the 1-1/8" and 1-3/8" holes need a bi-metal hole saw, since most step bits stop at 7/8".

Center-punch first so the bit does not wander, back the panel where you can, go slow with a little cutting oil, and deburr and touch up the paint on every hole. Bare steel at a cut edge is where rust starts, and this is a wet, sun-exposed spot.

The quad glands are the win here

Those 4-port Mikrotik glands put RS485, the TPDIN relay pair, and two spares through ONE hole instead of four. Three fewer penetrations, three fewer things to leak. Use one in Box B and save the other two for the irrigation control box, which will have a similar pile of small wires.

Glands or conduit?

Glands for the DC, conduit for the AC run

Glands on everything DC. Your 2/0, 6 ga, and PV wire all have jackets rated for exposed outdoor use, the runs are short and up off the ground, and a gland gives the seal and the strain relief in one part. 2/0 in conduit would need 1-1/4 to 1-1/2" for a pair, which is stiff, expensive, and as you found will not fit.

Conduit for the AC run to your office. That is a branch circuit crossing a steel penetration into a living space, so it wants real mechanical protection, and small wire pulls easily.

Conduit hubs are for pulling loose conductors (individual THWN). You are running jacketed cable, so glands are the natural match. Mixing both on one box is normal.

Rules that keep these watertight

  1. One cable per gland (the quad is the exception, one per port). Two cables crammed into one seal will not seal. Blank any port you are not using.
  2. Confirm every gland still has its locknut and gasket. NPT glands are made to thread into a tapped port; in a plain drilled hole the seal comes from the locknut plus the washer under the head. Parts pulled off a router often lose them.
  3. Drip loop below every gland. The cable should hang below the entry before it rises. Water follows cable, and the loop gives it a place to fall off.
  4. Tighten the compression nut on the cable, not just the body. You should not be able to pull the cable through by hand, and the rubber should visibly bite the jacket.
  5. Deburr and paint every hole. Painted steel, wet location, sun exposed.

Two things to keep an eye on

Your 1/2" stock is exactly consumed. Two glands for the two 6 ga Tycon leads, no spare. Nothing to buy, but do not strip one.

Test-fit the 2/0 before you drill 1-3/8". 2/0 jackets vary from about 0.60 to 0.70". A 1" NPT clamps roughly 0.51 to 0.75", so it should be comfortable, but confirm the rubber actually grips before committing that big a hole to that spot.

On running the 2/0 between the boxes

It would work mechanically, but that gap is where the lower box exhausts out its top face. It is already restricted by how close the boxes ended up, and that fan is what is buying you the reduced clearances inside both boxes.

Route the 2/0 up the outside right instead, into the two 1" glands low on Box A. If you do use the gap, keep it to one cable pushed to one side so the fan still has a path.

The other penetration: the wheel well

That one is 3/8" diamond plate, a different job. Bi-metal hole saw, slow speed, cutting oil, and deburr it thoroughly: a burr in 3/8" steel will saw through cable insulation over time. Size the hole to whatever lands there, either a 3/4" NPT gland (1-1/8") if you run jacketed cord, or a conduit fitting if you run conduit into the office.
